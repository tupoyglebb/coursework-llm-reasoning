# Two experiments for LLM replication: torture support and charitable giving

Below are two experiments selected for sharp contrast in socially sensitive judgment:
1. a **negative / hard-edge** case, where respondents may hide support for coercive violence;
2. a **positive / prosocial** case, where respondents may exaggerate willingness to act altruistically.

The formatting is designed so that each condition can be rendered as a complete prompt by concatenating `vignette + question + response_options_text`.

## 1. Hassner (2023) — *Persuasive and Unpersuasive Critiques of Torture*

**Theme:** support for torture / coercive interrogation.  
**Core judgment:** whether the respondent supports depriving a terror suspect of sleep during interrogation.  
**Treatment family:** moral vs pragmatic critique of torture.  
**Design:** a base scenario is held constant, while treatment arms add one of two kinds of information:
- a **pragmatic** cue that torture is unlikely to yield genuinely new intelligence,
- a **moral/cruelty** cue that the interrogation may last a month,
- or both together.  
**Why this is useful:** the experiment tests which kind of anti-torture argument actually changes public support. Hassner shows that respondents are largely unmoved by the “torture doesn’t work well” argument, but are much more responsive to information that makes the cruelty and protracted duration of torture vivid.  
**Key statistical result:** when respondents learn that interrogation may last a month, support for torture falls substantially—by about 14 percentage points in one survey and by about 30 percentage points in a second survey.  
**URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/persuasive-and-unpersuasive-critiques-of-torture/D01E3D4180E9DF607808CAD4E3059B43

## 2. Savary & Goldsmith (2020) — *Unobserved Altruism: How Self-Signaling Motivations and Social Benefits Shape Willingness to Donate*

**Theme:** willingness to donate / prosocial self-presentation.  
**Core judgment:** whether the respondent is willing to make a small charitable donation.  
**Treatment family:** private vs public giving, plus the value of social benefits attached to public recognition.  
**Design:** the charitable ask stays constant, but the request varies in whether the donation is:
- **private**,
- **publicly recognized**,
- or **publicly recognized with more valuable social benefits**.  
**Why this is useful:** this experiment is a strong “positive-pole” counterpart to torture support. Instead of hiding a harsh view, respondents may overstate their willingness to do something socially desirable. Savary and Goldsmith show that public recognition does not always increase willingness to donate; for small donations, it can actually reduce donation likelihood by making motives look less genuinely altruistic.  
**Key statistical result:** the paper reports robust evidence that public recognition can decrease donation likelihood, and that this negative effect attenuates when the social benefits of recognition become sufficiently valuable.  
**URL:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3026475
