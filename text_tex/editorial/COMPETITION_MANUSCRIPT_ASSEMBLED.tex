\begin{abstract}
We examine whether large language models (LLMs) reproduce human experimental treatment contrasts in socially sensitive survey vignettes and how system-prompt instructions change their answers. We extend an earlier two-case analysis of coercive interrogation and charitable giving with energy-conservation and bribery experiments, drawing on 6,000 valid additional synthetic responses from a partially balanced model--treatment--stimulus design. Behavioral correspondence with published human results is mixed. Some energy-conservation effects reproduce human directions but differ substantially in magnitude; the bribery reference condition produces only negative responses. Prompt effects vary by model and task: A and C raise investment willingness in GLM-4.6 but lower it in Kimi-K2-Thinking relative to neutral realism, while in the bribery task they alter affirmative responses and refusals, sometimes producing a stronger response to an institutional-inefficiency placebo than to corruption information. We distinguish shifts away from normatively approved answers from agreement with human treatment effects. Existing annotation and local hidden-state findings concern the original cases; annotation of the additional cases is ongoing and is not reported as completed. The results suggest that system prompts are consequential experimental inputs, rather than validated tools for correcting human-style social desirability bias.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to simulate survey respondents and to pilot experimental designs before human data collection \cite{argyle2023,horton2023,dillion2023}. Yet a model's ability to produce plausible answers does not establish that it reproduces the effects of an experimental manipulation. For research design, the more relevant question is whether a synthetic sample responds to treatment conditions in the same direction as human participants, and whether that correspondence survives changes in the instructions given to the model.

Socially sensitive vignette experiments provide a demanding test. Human answers can be shaped by social desirability pressures, while LLM outputs may also reflect safety alignment, preference optimization, and instruction-following \cite{tourangeau2007,krumpal2013,salecha2024,sharma2023}. These processes are not equivalent. In particular, a prompt that shifts an LLM answer away from a socially approved position does not necessarily make that answer more representative of human behavior. The distinction between \emph{behavioral steering} and \emph{human-effect replication} is therefore central to this study.

We examine LLM responses to four published experimental cases: Hassner's vignette on coercive interrogation, Savary and Goldsmith's study of charitable donation and public recognition, Bolsen and colleagues' energy-conservation framing experiment, and Corbacho and colleagues' corruption-information experiment. The last two cases extend the behavioral analysis across additional socially sensitive domains and outcome types. We compare treatment contrasts in synthetic responses with those reported for human respondents, and investigate how additional system-prompt stimuli affect model answers. The stimuli are treated as experimental interventions, not as validated corrections for social desirability bias.

\textbf{Research question.} How do system-prompt stimuli intended to alter the conditions of socially desirable responding affect LLM behavior in socially sensitive vignette experiments, as observed in final answers, verbalized reasoning, and internal representations?

\textbf{Goal.} To characterize the effects of system-prompt stimuli across three complementary observational levels: (1) final survey answers and their sensitivity to original vignette treatments; (2) the argumentative structure of verbalized reasoning; and (3) hidden-state representations in a separate local-model analysis.

\textbf{Objectives.} First, establish the behavioral baseline by comparing LLM treatment contrasts with published human benchmarks in the four experimental cases, where a comparable benchmark is available. Second, estimate how stimulus conditions and their design attributes affect answers and interact with vignette treatments. Third, analyze stimulus-associated differences in annotated reasoning and their relationship to answers. Fourth, explore the representation of stimulus, treatment, and answer information in hidden states. Human benchmarks provide an external reference for interpreting synthetic responses, not a universal success criterion for every analysis.

The three levels of observation are analytically distinct. Changes in verbalized reasoning do not by themselves identify causal cognitive mechanisms; local-model hidden-state findings cannot automatically be generalized to API models. Likewise, movement away from a socially approved answer does not establish reduced social desirability bias or greater human realism.

The behavioral evidence covers four cases, whereas the reasoning-annotation and local hidden-state findings reported here remain limited to the original studies. We distinguish newly analyzed behaviors from analyses not yet completed for the new reasoning traces. The paper first reviews the relevant literature and experimental designs, then presents the generation and analysis protocols, behavioral results, supplementary reasoning and representation analyses, and the limits of using LLMs for experimental pretesting.


\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{LLMs as synthetic respondents}

LLMs offer inexpensive ways to pilot surveys and experimental vignettes. Early work explored demographic conditioning and \emph{algorithmic fidelity} \cite{argyle2023}, classical experimental replication \cite{aher2023}, and simulated economic agents \cite{horton2023}. Their usefulness depends not only on plausible answers but on reproducing human treatment contrasts. Replication studies find some directional agreement alongside exaggerated magnitudes, compressed variation, and weaker correspondence in socially sensitive domains \cite{cui2025,bisbee2024,santurkar2023,rottger2024}. We therefore benchmark LLM treatment contrasts against published human studies rather than assuming that synthetic samples are representative.

\subsection{Social desirability and instruction sensitivity}

Social desirability bias (SDB) is response distortion associated with perceived acceptability. Survey theory distinguishes impression management from self-deceptive enhancement and emphasizes question sensitivity, privacy, and administration mode \cite{crowne1960,paulhus1984,tourangeau2007,krumpal2013}. Indirect elicitation and anonymity can reduce some human response pressures \cite{nederhof1985,horiuchi2022}; system prompts are not established equivalents of these survey methods.

LLM behavior introduces additional explanations. Models may favor socially approved answers, conform to perceived user expectations, or respond to safety-related training and instructions \cite{salecha2024,sharma2023}. Survey prompts and response formats can also change their outputs \cite{tjuatja2024}. A shift away from a socially approved answer therefore need not represent reduced human-like SDB. This motivates studying system-prompt stimuli as experimentally consequential inputs and distinguishing behavioral steering from correspondence with human treatment effects.

\subsection{Consequence framing versus gain--loss framing}

Human studies establish that framing can affect decisions, but the magnitude varies substantially across problem designs \cite{kuhberger1998}. Reviews of persuasive messages likewise do not find a general advantage of loss over gain framing \cite{okeefeJensen2006}. Importantly, those studies manipulate how a choice or message is presented to human respondents. Our N$-$/N$+$ conditions instead frame consequences in \emph{system-level instructions} to a synthetic respondent, rather than presenting matched gains and losses within the vignette. Their effects need not replicate classic gain--loss framing.

\subsection{Reasoning and internal representations}

Chain-of-thought prompting can alter generated outputs \cite{wei2022,kojima2022}, but verbalized reasoning is not a validated record of a model's internal causal process. We distinguish final answers, coded argumentative content, and hidden-state measurements. Annotation can identify associations between expressed arguments and responses, conditional on its reliability. Hidden-state probes offer a separate representational diagnostic; in our design, they concern a different local model and cannot be treated as a direct explanation of API-model behavior.

\subsection*{Research contribution}

This study compares original treatment effects and system-prompt effects across four adapted experimental domains, with published human contrasts as benchmarks. For the two original cases it also examines reasoning annotation and exploratory hidden-state evidence. The extension tests whether prompt sensitivity generalizes across models, normative contexts, and response formats without assuming that movement away from socially desirable answers improves human validity.


\section{Research Design and Data}

\subsection{Case selection and experimental treatments}

We study four published \emph{attitude-expression} vignette experiments. Unlike autobiographical self-report questions, these tasks ask for judgments about externally described situations. This choice permits controlled variation of vignette treatments without assigning demographic identities or personal histories to the model. The original cases also differ in the normative direction of the response: endorsement of coercive interrogation is socially disapproved, whereas charitable giving is socially approved. We use this contrast to investigate whether system-prompt stimuli have similar or domain-specific effects.

\textbf{Hassner: coercive interrogation.} Following \textcite{hassner2023}, models evaluate the use of sleep deprivation in interrogating a terrorism suspect. The four conditions are a base case, modest expected intelligence value, protracted duration, and their combination. The response is binary (support versus opposition). Opposition is treated as the \emph{normatively approved response direction} for descriptive comparisons, not as a verified measure of an individual respondent's latent attitude.

\textbf{Savary and Goldsmith: charitable donation.} Following \textcite{savary2020}, models report willingness to donate \$5 on a five-point scale. The three vignette conditions are private donation, public recognition, and public recognition combined with an additional social benefit. Greater stated willingness is the normatively approved response direction. The public-recognition treatment is also substantively important because it may alter the interpretation of donation as altruism or reputational signaling.

\paragraph{Bolsen et al.: energy conservation.}
The third experiment adapts the energy-conservation framing design of \textcite{bolsen2014}. Its nine treatment cells combine attribution of responsibility (government, individuals, or neither) with economic-cost, environmental-benefit, or no consequence framing. Models rate likelihood of investing in home insulation or weatherization on a seven-point scale. We examine original treatment-versus-control comparisons as well as attribution-by-consequence contrasts. The dependent variable measures stated willingness, not observed investment.

\paragraph{Corbacho et al.: bribery and institutional information.}
The fourth experiment adapts \textcite{corbacho2016}, comparing a control with information about corruption prevalence and an institutional-inefficiency placebo. Respondents indicate whether they would offer a bribe to avoid a fine, selecting True, False, or Prefer not to respond. These are three nominal outcomes; refusal is not interchangeable with unwillingness. The source paper reports direct-response and privacy-adjusted comparisons with different estimands. Our prompt adaptation does not reproduce its original crosswise-response procedure.

Across all four experiments, human source outcomes, stakes, and elicitation procedures differ, so directional comparisons are more defensible than pooling numerical effects across studies.

Published human treatment contrasts provide an external benchmark. They are not assumed to be reproduced by every model, and matching a treatment direction does not imply that the full human response distribution or the underlying psychological mechanism has been reproduced.

\subsection{Prompt structure and unit of observation}

The original vignette treatment enters through the user-facing question and response options. The system message specifies the survey-response task; experimental system-prompt stimuli are added to this instruction layer. No demographic persona is assigned in the principal behavioral design. The exact template and verbatim stimulus strings must be preserved from the generation configurations before the final manuscript is frozen.

Each generation produces a final answer and, where available, a model-generated reasoning trace associated with the same response event. These are separate analytical outputs, not independent observations. Repeated generations under a fixed model--experiment--treatment--stimulus configuration measure response variation within that configuration; they do not represent independent human respondents. We distinguish the \emph{run} (one generation) from the \emph{design cell} (a specified combination of model, vignette treatment, stimulus, and relevant generation settings). Statistical uncertainty must account for this nested structure rather than treating all completions as exchangeable people.

\subsection{Models, decoding, and analytical samples}

The behavioral corpus uses four API-accessed reasoning-capable models: \texttt{moonshotai/kimi-k2-thinking}, \texttt{z-ai/glm-4.6}, \texttt{qwen/qwen3.5-9b}, and \texttt{minimax/minimax-m2.5}. These models were selected pragmatically for the availability of final answers and reasoning traces; they do not constitute a representative sample of all LLMs. A separate local \texttt{Qwen3-8B} is used for hidden-state analysis and is not pooled with the API behavioral sample.

The principal generation regime uses temperature $T=0.01$, with a separate no-stimulus comparison at $T=1.0$. Low temperature improves repeatability but may compress within-cell variation; the temperature comparison is a decoding sensitivity check rather than an independent human benchmark.

The existing manuscript reports 15{,}680 raw runs, 12{,}434 retained rows after preprocessing, and 11{,}040 observations in the main $T=0.01$ analysis. These are \emph{reported historical totals}, not figures independently recomputed for this editorial draft. Final counts, exclusion rules, rerun handling, and cell-level coverage must be reconciled against raw data and analysis scripts before submission. In particular, the extended H--M stimulus family is not covered by all four API models; analyses of these conditions must identify the actual model subset and avoid unqualified cross-model generalization.

\section{System-Prompt Stimuli}

\subsection{Conceptual role}

System-prompt stimuli modify the instructions surrounding a fixed vignette. They are intended to discourage normatively polished or sanitized answers, but their observed effects are described here as \emph{stimulus-induced response shifts}, not as established reductions in social desirability bias. The direction of a shift is evaluated relative to the \emph{normatively approved response direction} in each case. A shift away from that direction does not, on its own, establish greater correspondence with human responses.

The historically implemented stimulus set includes the no-stimulus baseline, two core conditions (A and C), and the extended H--M family. A emphasizes the costs of sanitized answers; C includes an explicit anti-overcorrection qualification. The H--M family varies the stated target of consequences or benefits (science versus a company; personal or institutional benefit versus public good) and the valence of the framing (negative consequences versus positive benefits). These labels describe intended prompt functions, not independently identified causal mechanisms.

\subsection{Implemented stimulus conditions in the new experiments}
Bolsen and Corbacho each use A, C, N0, N$-$, and N$+$ prompts. N0 explicitly instructs the model to respond with neutral realism; it is \emph{not} the historical no-incentive condition. Similarly labeled prompts should be compared using the actual message text rather than their short identifiers alone. Therefore the new N0-referenced comparisons and the older unstimulated-baseline comparisons have different reference conditions, and are not treated as a fully matched four-experiment factorial experiment.

\subsection{Analytical contrasts}

The behavioral analysis distinguishes three comparisons: (i) original vignette-treatment contrasts within a fixed prompt condition; (ii) stimulus-induced shifts relative to a no-stimulus or explicitly specified reference condition; and (iii) changes in original treatment contrasts across stimulus conditions. Attribute-level comparisons are interpreted as conditional associations unless the compared prompts isolate the attribute of interest and have sufficient balanced coverage. Reasoning annotations and hidden-state measures are examined as complementary outcomes, with their separate validity and generalizability limitations stated in the relevant methods sections.

\section{Analytical Strategy}

We analyze three distinct levels of model response: final answers, verbalized reasoning, and hidden-state representations. The first two concern the four API models; the third is an exploratory analysis of a separate local model. The unit of recorded data is one generation, but repeated generations within a model--vignette-treatment--stimulus cell are not independent synthetic respondents.

\subsection{Final answers}

For Hassner, the binary support outcome is analyzed using a logit GLM; for Savary, the five-point willingness outcome is analyzed using OLS, with ordinal and thresholded specifications as sensitivity checks. The principal models include vignette-treatment, system-stimulus, and model indicators, plus treatment-by-stimulus interactions; an expanded specification also allows treatment effects to vary by model. Nonparametric comparisons use chi-squared tests for Hassner and rank-based tests for Savary. Holm corrections are applied to prespecified pairwise families, and false-discovery-rate adjustments are reported for broader coefficient families.

A treatment-by-stimulus interaction tests whether the original vignette contrast changes under a system-prompt stimulus. For two treatment levels and two stimulus conditions, the descriptive difference-in-differences contrast is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{DiD}}=
(\bar y_{1,s}-\bar y_{0,s})-(\bar y_{1,0}-\bar y_{0,0}).
\end{equation}
This is an interaction contrast within a controlled generation design, not a claim about a temporal policy intervention.

The original analysis reports heteroskedasticity-robust HC3 estimates and cluster-robust re-estimation by model $\times$ vignette treatment $\times$ stimulus (96 Hassner and 72 Savary clusters in the reported main specification). Because completions within a cell may be highly similar at $T=0.01$, cluster-aware inference is central to interpretation; naive run-level standard errors can overstate precision. Quasi-separation in binary outcomes motivates penalized-logit checks, and failure of proportional odds in Savary motivates retaining OLS and thresholded sensitivity analyses.

\subsection{Stimulus attributes}

An exploratory regression encodes the historically fielded stimulus conditions using indicators for framing valence, company versus scientific context, public versus personal beneficiary, and stimulus family, on the Kimi/GLM subsample where H--M were collected. These coefficients summarize conditional differences among \emph{observed prompt packages}. Since the H--M conditions are incomplete and not a fully crossed factorial, attribute coefficients should not be interpreted as isolated causal effects of valence, beneficiary, or recipient. The N0/N-negative/N-positive conditions are analyzed in the new Bolsen/Corbacho behavioral extension; they are separate from the historical estimates.

\subsection{Behavioral analysis of the additional experiments}
For Bolsen, model-specific treatment-cell means and complete ordinal distributions on the seven-point scale are reported alongside the original treatment contrasts and attribution-by-consequence interactions. Mean contrasts are checked against ordinal response distributions rather than interpreted as a universal location shift. For Corbacho, the primary outcome is the three-part distribution over True, False, and Prefer not to respond; refusal is preserved as its own response category. Binary True-versus-False comparisons conditional on supplying a substantive answer are sensitivity estimates, not substitutes for the full outcome.

For both cases, stimulus effects are compared within model against the neutral realism N0 condition, and treatment-by-stimulus interactions are described as differences of treatment contrasts. Cell-level effect sizes and model heterogeneity receive priority over run-level significance testing. Conditional multinomial and sequence-block resampling (block lengths five and ten) supply descriptive reference intervals, not valid sampling-based inference about a population of independent people. A constant observed design cell does not establish a structurally zero response probability.

\subsection{Annotation of verbalized reasoning}

The original annotation pipeline uses \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} to code generated reasoning traces with a fixed schema. Two disjoint batches of 5{,}040 traces yielded 10{,}080 annotations before merging and filtering; the manuscript reports 8{,}638 retained annotated records. The schema contains 19 causality fields (absent, mentioned, or decisive), 15 direction fields (negative, neutral, positive), and three binary style markers. Analyses use category presence and, where defined, signed presence. These labels describe \emph{verbalized text}; they are not direct measurements of latent reasoning mechanisms.

Annotation agreement was checked against expert manual coding on 27 traces, with substantial variation across categories. The small validation sample limits the precision and generalizability of reliability estimates. Results for weakly validated or rare categories are treated as exploratory rather than measurement-grade.

Category prevalence is modeled against vignette treatment, stimulus, and model identity, using logistic models for binary presence and linear models for signed presence. Multiple-comparison adjustments are applied across category-level tests. Associations between annotated features and final answers are assessed by comparing baseline outcome models with specifications that add annotation features; an additional fit omits the co-generated treatment-effect direction code to reduce tautological prediction. These regressions are descriptive associations, not evidence that a stated argument caused the answer.

A separability analysis compares classification of stimulus presence with classification of decoding temperature from annotation vectors, using regularized logistic regression and gradient boosting with stratified five-fold cross-validation and ROC-AUC. A sensitivity analysis removes features directly encoding stimulus awareness. TreeSHAP is used for exploratory feature interpretation, followed by explicit regression checks of selected associations; neither feature importance nor post-selection significance establishes a causal mechanism.

\subsection{Exploratory hidden-state analysis}

A separate local \texttt{Qwen3-8B} experiment records last-token hidden states at 37 layers (4{,}096 dimensions) for 98 runs: 56 Hassner and 42 Savary, across no stimulus and A/B/C/D/F/X. These conditions and model identity differ from the principal API corpus. Findings therefore concern the local implementation and are not direct measurements of hidden states in the four API models.

Layer-wise between-condition versus within-condition Euclidean distances are summarized by a separation ratio and a standardized mean-distance analogue. Linear centered kernel alignment (CKA) compares matched representation matrices across prompt conditions. A trajectory-length statistic sums successive layer-wise distances within a run, with paired Wilcoxon comparisons for matched conditions. Finally, layer-wise logistic probes assess stimulus and treatment information, while ridge probes assess answer decodability, including a condition-residualized check. Cross-validation and matched comparisons reduce some forms of leakage, but the small sample and high-dimensional representations make this track exploratory. Decodability and geometric separation are not, by themselves, evidence of causal mediation.

\subsection{Interpretive scope}

We distinguish (i) socially desirable response patterns, (ii) stimulus-induced response shifts, and (iii) correspondence with published human treatment contrasts. None is a substitute for the others. Evidence that a stimulus moves an answer away from the normatively approved pole does not establish that it mitigates human-style social desirability bias. Across all three tracks, the analysis identifies observable differences and statistical associations; mechanistic and human-validity claims require additional evidence.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Final answers: heterogeneous models and systematic prompt shifts}

The four API models differ substantially in observed answers. Across the reported $T=0.01$ analytical sample, Hassner endorsement rates range from 10.3\% (Minimax) to 67.5\% (GLM-4.6), while Savary mean willingness ranges from 3.33 (Kimi) to 3.73 (Qwen). The Hassner treatment-by-model specification also improves model fit (McFadden pseudo-$R^2$: $0.507$ to $0.543$; $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=-304$). Model identity therefore matters for both response levels and sensitivity to vignette manipulations. These descriptive rates pool different available stimulus conditions and should not be read as balanced model comparisons.

In the no-stimulus Hassner condition, support for sleep deprivation decreases from 44.6\% in the control to 19.3\% with protracted duration, 12.2\% with modest intelligence value, and 1.0\% with both modifications. These directions agree with the human experimental benchmark. In Savary, by contrast, the LLM mean rises from 3.39 in the private condition to 3.73 with public recognition and 4.09 with added social benefit. The increment associated with social benefit is directionally consistent with the source experiment, but the private-to-public increase reverses the human finding: public recognition \emph{reduced} willingness in the original study. The benchmark therefore reveals domain-specific limits of directional replication rather than a general success or failure of synthetic respondents (Figure~\ref{fig:replication}).

All eight historical system-prompt stimuli are associated with shifts \emph{away from the normatively approved response direction} in the pooled descriptive comparisons. In Hassner, the no-stimulus endorsement rate is 19.1\%; it is 41.6\% under C, 72.1\% under A, and ranges from 56.4\% to 90.0\% across H--M. In Savary, mean willingness is 3.74 without a stimulus, 3.44 under C, 3.32 under A, and 3.14--3.35 across H--M (Table~\ref{tab:incentive-means}; Figure~\ref{fig:stimuli-effects}). These are \emph{socially desirable response patterns} and \emph{stimulus-induced response shifts}, not direct estimates of human-style SDB or its mitigation.

The comparisons require a coverage qualification: H--M were fielded only on Kimi and GLM-4.6, while the baseline and core conditions have broader model coverage. Thus the displayed pooled means are useful descriptive summaries, but differences involving H--M are not automatically like-for-like causal estimates. In the historical attribute regression restricted to the two-model subsample, positive-framed L/M prompts are associated with substantially higher Hassner endorsement than negative-framed H--K (reported OR $\approx15$, $p<0.001$) and a smaller decrease in Savary willingness (reported $\beta=-0.13$, $p=0.02$). Because the observed prompts do not form a complete matched factorial, these are associations between prompt packages, not isolated effects of framing valence. Nonsignificant recipient and beneficiary coefficients do not establish that those attributes are inert.

Treatment-by-stimulus contrasts also vary: the original analysis reports moderation in some conditions, without reversal of the sign of the original Hassner treatment contrasts. In Savary, the private-to-public contrast remains positive across the eight historical stimuli (reported range $+0.05$ to $+0.26$), so none reproduces the human reversal under the tested settings. This result identifies a limitation of the \emph{observed prompts}, not a general impossibility of eliciting self-signaling-related reasoning.

\paragraph{Historical-data reconciliation and model-specific sensitivity.}
A versioned reconstruction of historical Notebook 05 preprocessing yields 11,040 main $T=0.01$ observations in 168 observed model--treatment--stimulus cells (6,454 Hassner; 4,586 Savary), compared with 12,838 valid rows in an earlier aggregate export using different exclusion and rerun rules. Of the 168 cells, 50 differ in valid sample size and 16 in estimated outcome value. The pooled historical baseline means reported above agree with the Notebook 05 reconstruction, but previously circulated model-specific contrasts should not be combined with them indiscriminately.

For the two principal API models, the reconstructed no-stimulus treatment-minus-control contrasts are $-0.785$ (GLM) and $-0.442$ (Kimi) for modest intelligence, and $-0.438$ (GLM) and $-0.539$ (Kimi) for prolonged duration in Hassner. For Savary, the public-minus-private contrasts are $+0.238$ (GLM) and $+0.400$ (Kimi); social-benefit-minus-public increments are $+0.435$ and $+0.492$. Thus the original directional interpretation is unchanged while some GLM effect magnitudes require revision. These values are point-estimate reconciliations, not re-estimated regression significance tests.

The reconstruction reproduces Notebook 05's preprocessing on an available master-table copy, without proving byte identity with its inaccessible original input. It also flags 753 valid rows whose run-directory name suggests zero temperature while recorded metadata indicate $T=0.01$. Excluding that run leaves the main directions for GLM and Kimi unchanged; actual request-temperature provenance remains unresolved.

\subsection{Fresh no-additional-stimulus replication for Hassner and Savary}

An additional October 2026 run evaluates both historical vignettes under \texttt{none}, i.e., no appended Incentive section while retaining the baseline system instruction to respond as a plausible survey participant. Of 700 planned generations, 698 yield strictly valid parsed responses at $T=0.01$. The only exclusions are one malformed Kimi Hassner control response (\texttt{1. Yes}) and one empty GLM Hassner duration response that terminated at the length limit. These results are a distinct run, rather than rows silently pooled into the original historical summary.

For Hassner, GLM's control endorsement rate is 1.000 ($n=50$), versus 0.673 for prolonged duration ($n=49$), 0.360 for modest intelligence value ($n=50$), and 0.040 for both changes ($n=50$). Kimi's corresponding rates are 0.571 ($n=49$), 0.140 ($n=50$), 0.000 ($n=50$), and 0.000 ($n=50$). All original treatments therefore reduce endorsement relative to control in both models, corroborating the directional pattern from the earlier corpus but not its exact response levels. Restoring the malformed Kimi control answer as Yes changes that control estimate only from 0.571 to 0.580.

For Savary, GLM's mean giving willingness is 3.480 ($n=50$) in private, 3.920 ($n=50$) with public recognition, and 4.420 ($n=50$) with added social benefit. Kimi's means are 3.340, 3.660, and 4.000 (all $n=50$). Thus public recognition increases willingness by 0.440 points in GLM and 0.320 points in Kimi relative to private giving; this again runs opposite to the reported original human public-recognition direction.

The additional none run has mixed inference-provider routing across models, and its reported ordinary bootstrap intervals describe variation among API completions, not population uncertainty. It does not establish the effects of N0, N$-$, or N$+$: the accompanying report does not contain the corresponding runs. No none-versus-N-stimulus contrasts are computed from this file, and historical observations are not pooled with the new run without verifying the generation protocols.

\subsection{Additional experiments: Bolsen and Corbacho}

\paragraph{Sample and comparability.}
The two new experiments contain 6,034 saved generations, 6,000 of which satisfy parsing and scale checks. GLM-4.6 and Kimi-K2-Thinking cover the full treatment-by-stimulus design; Qwen3.5-9B contributes only 33 valid Bolsen control/A responses and is excluded from the principal comparisons. The realized prompt conditions are A, C, N0, N$-$, and N$+$. N0 is a neutral realism instruction, not the historical no-incentive baseline; these references must not be equated.

\paragraph{Bolsen: partial directional replication and model heterogeneity.}
Under N0, both models' control means are 5.000 on the seven-point investment scale. Government attribution lowers investment likelihood by 0.840 points for GLM and 1.800 for Kimi, compared with approximately $-0.370$ in the published human contrast. Cost framing lowers it by 1.060 and 0.600 points, compared with a published contrast of approximately $-0.360$. The combined individual-attribution and environmental-benefit condition is only 0.020 points above control for either model, versus approximately $+0.450$ in the human study. Agreement in sign thus coexists with sizeable discrepancies in magnitude (Table~\ref{tab:bolsen-new-contrasts}). Published human contrasts are reconstructed from rounded means, and exact comparability is limited.

The factorial pattern is also heterogeneous. At N0, the individual-by-environment interaction is 0.000 for GLM and $-0.180$ for Kimi, against approximately $+0.120$ in the reported human means. Averaging equally across the nine treatments, A changes GLM's mean by $+0.168$ relative to N0 but Kimi's by $-0.665$; the corresponding C shifts are $+0.257$ and $-0.097$. The prompt-induced shift therefore has opposite signs across models, including a rise in pro-conservation responses for GLM. In Kimi, the cost-minus-control contrast changes from $-0.600$ under N0 to $+0.080$ under A. These shifts neither guarantee greater human correspondence nor support a uniform de-sanitization claim.

\paragraph{Corbacho: three response outcomes and placebo sensitivity.}
Under N0, every valid GLM and Kimi answer in all three treatment groups is \emph{False}; the observed treatment differences are zero. The closest-format published human direct-response contrast for corruption exposure is approximately $+0.030$, with a reported confidence interval reaching approximately zero. A larger published privacy-adjusted joint contrast ($+0.080$) measures a different quantity and cannot be substituted. Human and synthetic elicitation procedures differ.

For Kimi under A, the proportions choosing True are 0.560 in control, 0.694 under corruption information, and 0.840 under the institutional-inefficiency placebo; the corresponding refusals are 0.380, 0.306, and 0.160. Under C, True proportions are 0.100, 0.300, and 0.447, with refusals of 0.440, 0.220, and 0.234. Thus the corruption-minus-placebo True contrast is approximately $-0.146$ under A and $-0.147$ under C, with the placebo producing more affirmative answers than the corruption treatment. The treatment-balanced True proportion under A is 0.054 for GLM but 0.698 for Kimi; no GLM refusals appear under A. Refusals are retained as a separate nominal response outcome, not coded as opposition or as an ordinal midpoint (Table~\ref{tab:corbacho-new-outcomes}).

\paragraph{The new negative- and positive-consequence stimuli.}
The N$-$ and N$+$ comparisons against N0 are much smaller than A/C effects in these two new cases (Table~\ref{tab:new-consequence-stimuli}). In Bolsen, treatment-balanced mean shifts under N$-$ are $+0.038$ (GLM) and $+0.104$ (Kimi) scale points, compared with $-0.014$ and $-0.011$ under N$+$. Thus the N$+$ minus N$-$ contrasts are $-0.051$ and $-0.115$, respectively. Under conditional block-of-ten resampling, the latter contrast has a reference interval of $[-0.186,-0.045]$ for Kimi but $[-0.112,0.008]$ for GLM; these are not human-population confidence intervals. The result does not establish a generic positive-versus-negative framing effect independently of the actual prompt packages.

In Corbacho, GLM has no observed True or refusal responses under either N$-$ or N$+$ in the balanced comparisons. Kimi's True share changes by $+0.033$ (3.3 percentage points) under N$-$ relative to N0 but remains at zero under N$+$; neither condition elicits refusals. Hence N$+$ minus N$-$ in Kimi is $-0.033$ for True, with a conditional block-of-ten reference interval $[-0.053,-0.013]$. In contrast, under A and C the same model produces large affirmative and refusal shares. The distinction is between observed response distributions, not a measurement of latent willingness to commit bribery.

\paragraph{Four-case interpretation.}
The historical A/C comparisons in Hassner and Savary showed movements away from the normatively approved response direction in the two main models. Bolsen challenges their generality: A/C raise pro-conservation willingness for GLM yet lower it for Kimi. Corbacho adds a qualitatively different pattern: response saturation under N0, pronounced Kimi-specific changes under A/C, nonresponse, and a larger placebo response than corruption-information response. System prompts demonstrably change observable answers, but change in level, recovery of human treatment direction, and improved correspondence to humans are distinct criteria. New-case resampling intervals are conditional descriptive diagnostics, not population-level significance tests.

\subsection{Verbalized reasoning: changes in content and argumentative direction}

The annotation results indicate that stimuli change the text models produce while selecting answers. Explicit \texttt{overcorrection\_concern} appears in approximately 9\% of no-stimulus traces and 72--76\% under A/C (expert-validation $\kappa=0.64$, $n=27$). The prevalence of explicitly rational-deliberative style rises from 0.67 to 0.86, and acknowledgement of multiple defensible positions from 0.57 to 0.80. The latter style codes were not included in the small human-validation set and should be treated as exploratory. These changes describe generated text, not independently verified cognitive awareness.

The A--C comparison is especially informative in Hassner. Endorsement is 72.1\% under A versus 41.6\% under C, while several coded category-prevalence differences are small. Direction codes, however, differ markedly: signed presence of \texttt{ratio\_of\_goals\_and\_means} changes from $-0.01$ under C to $+0.42$ under A, \texttt{majority\_reliance} from $+0.06$ to $+0.41$, and \texttt{cruelty} from $-0.68$ to $-0.34$ (Table~\ref{tab:direction-AvsC}). The first of these codes was not covered by expert validation; the others have limited or category-specific reliability evidence. The pattern is consistent with a shift in the \emph{polarity of verbalized arguments}, but it does not show that these arguments mediate the stimulus effect.

In Savary, both A and C strongly increase annotation of personal costs and benefits, while their mean final answers remain comparatively close (3.32 and 3.44). Across both domains, the annotation vocabulary therefore responds to prompts, but the relation between coded text and the answer is domain-dependent.

A separability benchmark classifies stimulus-present versus no-stimulus traces from annotation features at reported cross-validated ROC-AUC $0.96$, compared with $0.68$ for low- versus high-temperature no-stimulus traces (Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark}). The original analysis also reports that the contrast survives exclusion of directly stimulus-linked codes. This demonstrates that the two classification tasks have different separability under the chosen features and samples; it does not by itself measure relative causal impact on internal reasoning.

The annotation instrument has uneven validation. In a 27-trace expert-coded subset, agreement is high for \texttt{cruelty} and \texttt{personal\_cost\_burden} (both $\kappa=1.00$ for causality coding) and moderate-to-substantial for \texttt{overcorrection\_concern} ($\kappa=0.64$), whereas several psychological categories have near-zero agreement (Table~\ref{tab:kappa}). Interpretations of subtle self-presentational categories must therefore remain tentative.

\subsection{Associations between reasoning annotations and answers}

Adding annotation features increases reported explanatory fit in Savary from $R^2=0.419$ to $0.482$ ($\Delta R^2=0.063$). Overcorrection concern is negatively associated with willingness ($\beta=-0.047$, $p=0.012$), while positively directed personal-cost and private-interest codes are associated with higher willingness (both reported $\beta=+0.046$). A gradient-boosting model also outperforms the linear benchmark in cross-validation (reported $R^2=0.558$ versus $0.475$). These patterns indicate predictive information in verbalized reasoning beyond the specified treatment, stimulus, and model controls, but do not demonstrate that the generated text is causally upstream of the answer.

For Hassner, the original non-tautological annotation specification reports a smaller increment in fit ($\Delta R^2=0.052$). Positive directions of majority-reliance and means--ends arguments are associated with endorsement, while overcorrection concern itself is not a significant predictor ($p=0.92$). Taken together, the two experiments suggest that the \emph{observable association} between reasoning categories and final responses varies by domain. The small expert-validation sample and co-generation of traces and answers limit stronger process claims.

\subsection{Exploratory hidden-state results}

The local Qwen3-8B analysis covers 98 runs (56 Hassner and 42 Savary) under a different stimulus inventory from the API study. In matched comparisons, the reported cumulative hidden-state trajectory is shorter under stimulus conditions than without a stimulus (3{,}370 versus 3{,}444 distance units; paired Wilcoxon $p<0.001$; Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory}). This is a geometric difference across layer representations, not a direct measure of computational effort or deliberation.

The between-/within-condition separation statistic peaks near layers 20--21 in the reported comparisons. Linear CKA remains above 0.94 across layers (reported L20 CKA $=0.994$), indicating broadly similar representational geometry despite detectable condition differences (Figure~\ref{fig:separation}). These summaries do not uniquely identify a translation mechanism or a semantic processing stage.

The local model also exhibits a representational--behavioral dissociation: stimulus-related hidden-state differences are detectable even where its Hassner responses are near-saturated (reported $P(\mathrm{Yes})\approx0.998$). A treatment-status probe performs poorly under leave-one-group-out validation despite higher standard cross-validation performance, cautioning against scenario-specific leakage. For Savary, a condition-residualized ridge probe reaches a reported leave-one-out $R^2=0.665$ at layer 27 (Figure~\ref{fig:decodability}). The distinct layer peaks are descriptive: they do not establish when a decision is causally formed. Small samples, high-dimensional activations, and the separate model architecture constrain generalization.

\subsection{Synthesis}

Three results are most robust as a descriptive account of the historical corpus. First, model responses vary substantially across model families and between the two social domains; the Savary public-recognition contrast reverses the human benchmark. Second, system-prompt stimuli consistently accompany shifts away from the normatively approved response direction in the reported descriptive comparisons, while their apparent magnitude depends on prompt condition and model coverage. Third, stimuli are associated with changes in verbalized reasoning and with measurable hidden-state differences in a separate local model, but neither track alone identifies the mechanism producing final answers.

The historical findings support the study of system prompts as consequential experimental inputs, not as neutral wrappers. They do \emph{not} establish that steering responses away from social approval makes LLMs more human-like, that the observed attribute associations isolate framing valence, or that verbalized and representational measures constitute a causal chain. The additional Bolsen and Corbacho results above reveal further model- and domain-specific heterogeneity.

\section{Discussion}

Across four adapted vignette experiments, LLM treatment sensitivity varies by domain, model, and surrounding system instructions. Under the historical no-stimulus condition, Hassner treatment contrasts have the same direction as in the human study, whereas the Savary public-recognition contrast has the opposite sign. Bolsen shows partial directional correspondence but often different magnitudes. Corbacho produces uniformly negative answers under the neutral-realism reference N0; the corresponding human direct-response contrast is small and imprecisely estimated, limiting claims about replication failure.

The original A/C stimuli move Hassner and Savary answers away from the normatively approved response direction. This pattern is not general across the additional cases: relative to N0, A raises Bolsen energy-conservation willingness in GLM but lowers it in Kimi. In Corbacho, A/C change Kimi's affirmative and refusal shares substantially, and the institutional-inefficiency placebo elicits more affirmative answers than the corruption-information treatment. The new N$-$/N$+$ shifts are generally much smaller. These patterns establish prompt sensitivity, not greater fidelity to humans or a uniform reduction in social desirability bias. N0 includes a realism instruction and is not equivalent to historical no stimulus.

Classical gain--loss framing effects therefore do not imply that consequence framing in a system prompt must shift synthetic answers. The generally small N$-$/N$+$ contrasts are compatible with context-dependent framing effects, but do not establish equivalence or prove a zero effect. Saturated N0 responses in Corbacho also limit observable shifts, while N0 itself contains a realism instruction. Historical H--M comparisons suggest a valence association, but their incomplete prompt factorial cannot isolate valence causally. Reasoning annotations also vary in content and local argumentative direction across prompts, yet jointly generated traces and answers cannot establish mediation. Exploratory hidden-state differences in a separate local Qwen3-8B likewise cannot explain the API models' responses. LLM simulations may help stress-test experimental wording, but comparisons against human treatment benchmarks remain necessary.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

The four cases cover selected socially sensitive vignettes, not representative human survey populations. Model coverage is unequal: the historical H--M family is limited to GLM and Kimi, and the principal new-experiment comparisons likewise use those two models. Repeated low-temperature generations are not independent human respondents. Corbacho's three nominal answers require separate treatment of refusal; its human privacy-adjusted estimate is not directly equivalent to our direct-response outcome. The N0 reference and historical no-stimulus baseline differ, as do some original vignette procedures.

The historical reasoning codebook was checked against only 27 expert-coded traces, with highly uneven agreement across categories. Newly planned Bolsen/Corbacho reasoning annotation is not yet part of these results. Reasoning-answer associations are predictive, not causal. Hidden-state measurements concern 98 runs of a different, local Qwen3-8B checkpoint; layer-wise geometry and probing are diagnostic rather than mechanistic proof. Historical H--M prompt wording has not been independently authenticated verbatim. Finally, the historical numerical reconciliation uses an available master-table copy and does not establish identity with the original notebook input.

\section{Conclusion}

System prompts materially affect synthetic experimental answers, but the direction and implications of those effects depend on task and model. Agreement with some human treatment contrasts coexists with reversals, attenuated effects, response saturation, and placebo sensitivity. Historical traces and separate-model representations provide complementary correlational evidence, not a validated causal pathway. Consequently, moving answers away from socially approved positions must not be equated with improved human realism. LLMs are potentially useful for experimental stress-testing, but not as unvalidated substitutes for human respondents.
