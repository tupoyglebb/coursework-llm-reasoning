\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{LLMs as synthetic respondents and experimental subjects}

Large language models have been proposed as low-cost environments for piloting surveys and behavioral experiments. \textcite{argyle2023} introduced the notion of \emph{algorithmic fidelity}, using demographic conditioning to approximate human subgroup responses. \textcite{aher2023} extended simulation to classic human-subject experiments, while \textcite{horton2023} framed LLMs as computational economic agents. Related studies examine model behavior in repeated games and other experimental settings \cite{akata2025,mei2024}. Together, this literature establishes the methodological appeal of synthetic respondents: prompts and experimental conditions can be varied rapidly before human fieldwork.

A central distinction, however, is between matching an observed response distribution and recovering the \emph{effect} of an experimental treatment. In a large-scale replication of published vignette studies, \textcite{cui2025} reported frequent agreement in the direction of main effects, alongside inflated effect magnitudes and weaker performance on socially sensitive topics. Other studies document limited demographic responsiveness, compressed response variation, and sensitivity to question wording or answer-option format \cite{santurkar2023,bisbee2024,dominguez2024,rottger2024}. These findings caution against treating a plausible synthetic answer, or even agreement on one treatment contrast, as general evidence of human representativeness.

Our study therefore uses published human experiments as external behavioral benchmarks while investigating an additional question: how the \emph{instructions surrounding} an otherwise fixed vignette change LLM responses. The comparison with human treatment effects is informative, but the effect of system-prompt stimuli is also an object of study in its own right.

\subsection{Social desirability bias in survey research}

Social desirability bias (SDB) refers to response distortion associated with the perceived social acceptability of an answer. Classic accounts distinguish \emph{impression management}, in which respondents adjust their presentation to an audience, from \emph{self-deceptive enhancement}, which concerns favorable self-beliefs \cite{crowne1960,paulhus1984,randallFernandes1991}. Survey research further shows that sensitivity depends on the question, mode of administration, perceived privacy, and normative context \cite{tourangeau2007,krumpal2013}. Social desirability pressures can favor both the rejection of stigmatized attitudes and the endorsement of approved prosocial intentions.

These insights motivate the use of two attitude-expression vignettes with contrasting normative contexts: support for coercive interrogation and willingness to donate. Unlike autobiographical self-reports, these tasks ask respondents to evaluate described situations. This makes them suitable for comparing treatment responses under controlled prompt templates, although it does not establish that the resulting variation is caused by SDB alone.

Survey methodology offers both indirect elicitation designs and contextual measures, such as anonymity and self-administration, to limit socially desirable responding \cite{nederhof1985,horiuchi2022}. Our system-prompt stimuli are not equivalent to these validated human-survey techniques. They manipulate the instructions and purported stakes of the model's task; whether they reduce a bias, rather than merely redirect the answer, must be evaluated separately.

\subsection{Alignment, sycophancy, and prompt sensitivity}

The analogy between human SDB and LLM behavior is useful but incomplete. \textcite{salecha2024} document socially desirable shifts in models' personality-test answers, while \textcite{sharma2023} examine \emph{sycophancy}: the tendency of preference-trained models to favor responses aligned with apparent user expectations. These observations are compatible with normatively skewed answers in sensitive survey tasks, but do not identify a single mechanism shared by humans and models. Safety-related constraints, learned response preferences, and direct compliance with instructions are distinct possible explanations.

Prompt sensitivity further complicates interpretation. \textcite{tjuatja2024} report that aligned models can diverge from human survey-response biases while remaining sensitive to seemingly minor changes in wording. Consequently, a stimulus that moves an answer away from the socially approved pole may reflect instruction-following, framing effects, or other model-specific behavior. Such movement is an observable treatment effect, not by itself evidence of greater realism or an unbiased latent preference.

This motivates treating system-prompt wording as an experimentally manipulated factor rather than a neutral setup condition. The present study examines stimulus contrasts and attributes, including framing valence and the stated consequences of an answer, while keeping the distinction between \emph{response steering} and \emph{human correspondence} explicit.

\subsection{Reasoning traces and levels of observation}

Prompting can affect not only a final answer but also the text generated before it. Work on chain-of-thought elicitation shows that instructions to articulate intermediate steps can change task performance \cite{wei2022,kojima2022}; socially oriented reasoning prompts have likewise been studied in behavioral games \cite{akata2025}. These findings justify analyzing verbalized reasoning as an additional \emph{output} of the experimental condition. They do not establish that such traces faithfully reveal the internal causal process producing an answer.

We therefore distinguish three observational levels. First, final answers show behavioral responses to vignette treatments and system-prompt stimuli. Second, structured annotation of verbalized reasoning describes how generated arguments vary across conditions and relate statistically to answers; the interpretation depends on annotation reliability. Third, a separate local-model analysis explores whether stimulus-related differences are detectable in hidden-state representations. These analyses are complementary, but findings from the local model cannot automatically be attributed to the API models used in the behavioral experiments.

\subsection*{Research gap}

Existing research has examined synthetic respondent fidelity, social desirability and sycophancy in aligned models, and prompt-based behavioral steering. The present study connects these questions by systematically varying system-prompt stimuli in two socially sensitive vignette experiments and examining their effects across answers, verbalized reasoning, and---for a separate local model---hidden states. Human experimental results provide an external benchmark for interpreting behavioral contrasts, while the primary contribution is a multi-level account of how task-framing interventions alter LLM outputs and representations. Claims about bias mitigation, human validity, and causal mechanisms are kept separate from the directly observed effects.
