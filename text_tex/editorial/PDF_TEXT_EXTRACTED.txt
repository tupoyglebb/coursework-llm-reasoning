Abstract
This study examines how system-prompt instructions affect large language models (LLMs) used as synthetic respondents in four vignette experiments: coercive
interrogation, charitable giving, energy conservation, and bribery. We compare synthetic responses with published human treatment contrasts and analyze ﬁnal answers, verbalized reasoning, and exploratory hidden-state representations. Replication of human effects is uneven: some treatment directions agree, while others differ in sign or magnitude. System-prompt effects vary substantially across tasks and
models. Direct anti-sanitization instructions can produce large shifts in responses
and annotated argumentative content, whereas positive and negative framings of
research consequences generally yield smaller and less consistent changes. Reasoning categories are associated with ﬁnal answers, but direction-of-argument effects
are less robust, and neither verbalized reasoning nor hidden-state differences establish a causal mechanism. Overall, steering answers away from socially approved
positions does not reliably improve correspondence with human experimental effects. The ﬁndings underscore the importance of model identity, prompt design,
and human benchmarks when using LLMs for experimental research.
The authors used AI to assist with code development, data analysis workﬂows,
data markup and to improve the language and translation of the text. The authors
reviewed and edited the output and take full responsibility for the content.

1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to simulate survey
respondents and to pilot experimental designs before human data collection [Argyle et al., 2023; Dillion et al., 2023; Horton et al., 2023]. Yet a model’s ability
to produce plausible answers does not establish that it reproduces the effects of
an experimental manipulation. For research design, the more relevant question is
whether a synthetic sample responds to treatment conditions in the same direction
as human participants, and whether that correspondence survives changes in the
instructions given to the model.
Socially sensitive vignette experiments provide a demanding test. Human
answers can be shaped by social desirability pressures, while LLM outputs may
also reﬂect safety alignment, preference optimization, and instruction-following
[Krumpal, 2013; Salecha et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2024; Tourangeau & Yan,
2007]. These processes are not equivalent. In particular, a prompt that shifts an
LLM answer away from a socially approved position does not necessarily make
3

that answer more representative of human behavior. The distinction between behavioral steering and human-effect replication is therefore central to this study.
We examine LLM responses to four published experimental cases: Hassner’s vignette on coercive interrogation, Savary and Goldsmith’s study of charitable donation and public recognition, Bolsen and colleagues’ energy-conservation
framing experiment, and Corbacho and colleagues’ corruption-information experiment. The last two cases extend the behavioral analysis across additional socially
sensitive domains and outcome types. We compare treatment contrasts in synthetic responses with those reported for human respondents, and investigate how
additional system-prompt stimuli affect model answers. The stimuli are treated
as experimental interventions, not as validated corrections for social desirability
bias.
Research question. How do system-prompt stimuli intended to alter the
conditions of socially desirable responding affect LLM behavior in socially sensitive vignette experiments, as observed in ﬁnal answers, verbalized reasoning, and
internal representations?
Goal. To characterize the effects of system-prompt stimuli across three complementary observational levels: (1) ﬁnal survey answers and their sensitivity to
original vignette treatments; (2) the argumentative structure of verbalized reasoning; and (3) hidden-state representations in a separate local-model analysis.
Objectives. First, establish the behavioral baseline by comparing LLM
treatment contrasts with published human benchmarks in the four experimental
cases, where a comparable benchmark is available. Second, estimate how stimulus conditions and their design attributes affect answers and interact with vignette
treatments. Third, analyze stimulus-associated differences in annotated reasoning
and their relationship to answers. Fourth, explore the representation of stimulus,
treatment, and answer information in hidden states. Human benchmarks provide
an external reference for interpreting synthetic responses, not a universal success
criterion for every analysis.
The three levels of observation are analytically distinct. Changes in verbalized reasoning do not by themselves identify causal cognitive mechanisms; localmodel hidden-state ﬁndings cannot automatically be generalized to API models.
Likewise, movement away from a socially approved answer does not establish
reduced social desirability bias or greater human realism.
4

The behavioral evidence covers four cases, whereas the reasoningannotation and local hidden-state ﬁndings reported here remain limited to the
original studies. We analyze behavioral and reasoning evidence across all four

experimental pretesting.

2. Literature Review
2.1. LLMs as synthetic respondents
LLMs offer inexpensive ways to pilot surveys and experimental vignettes.
Early work explored demographic conditioning and algorithmic ﬁdelity [Argyle
et al., 2023], classical experimental replication [Aher et al., 2023], and simulated
economic agents [Horton et al., 2023]. Their usefulness depends not only on plausible answers but on reproducing human treatment contrasts. Replication studies
ﬁnd some directional agreement alongside exaggerated magnitudes, compressed
variation, and weaker correspondence in socially sensitive domains [Bisbee et al.,
2024; Cui et al., 2025; Röttger et al., 2024; Santurkar et al., 2023]. We therefore
benchmark LLM treatment contrasts against published human studies rather than
assuming that synthetic samples are representative.
2.2. Social desirability and instruction sensitivity
Social desirability bias (SDB) is response distortion associated with perceived acceptability. Survey theory distinguishes impression management from
self-deceptive enhancement and emphasizes question sensitivity, privacy, and administration mode [Crowne & Marlowe, 1960; Krumpal, 2013; Paulhus, 1984;
Tourangeau & Yan, 2007]. Indirect elicitation and anonymity can reduce some human response pressures [Horiuchi et al., 2022; Nederhof, 1985]; system prompts
are not established equivalents of these survey methods.
LLM behavior introduces additional explanations. Models may favor socially approved answers, conform to perceived user expectations, or respond to
safety-related training and instructions [Salecha et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2024].
5

Survey prompts and response formats can also change their outputs [Tjuatja et al.,
2024]. A shift away from a socially approved answer therefore need not represent reduced human-like SDB. This motivates studying system-prompt stimuli as
experimentally consequential inputs and distinguishing behavioral steering from
correspondence with human treatment effects.
2.3. Consequence framing versus gain–loss framing
Human studies establish that framing can affect decisions, but the magnitude varies substantially across problem designs [Kühberger, 1998]. Reviews of
persuasive messages likewise do not ﬁnd a general advantage of loss over gain
framing [O’Keefe & Jensen, 2006]. Importantly, those studies manipulate how a
choice or message is presented to human respondents. Our N−/N+ conditions
instead frame consequences in system-level instructions to a synthetic respondent,
rather than presenting matched gains and losses within the vignette. Their effects
need not replicate classic gain–loss framing.
2.4. Reasoning and internal representations
Chain-of-thought prompting can alter generated outputs [Kojima et al.,
2022; Wei et al., 2022], but verbalized reasoning is not a validated record of
a model’s internal causal process. We distinguish ﬁnal answers, coded argumentative content, and hidden-state measurements. Annotation can identify
associations between expressed arguments and responses, conditional on its
reliability. Hidden-state probes offer a separate representational diagnostic; in
our design, they concern a different local model and cannot be treated as a direct
explanation of API-model behavior.
Research contribution
This study compares original treatment effects and system-prompt effects
across four adapted experimental domains, with published human contrasts
as benchmarks. It examines verbalized reasoning across all four cases and
exploratory hidden-state representations for the two initial cases. The extension
tests whether prompt sensitivity generalizes across models, normative contexts,
and response formats without assuming that movement away from socially
6

desirable answers improves human validity.

3. Research Design and Data
3.1. Case selection and experimental treatments
We study four published attitude-expression vignette experiments. Unlike
autobiographical self-report questions, these tasks ask for judgments about externally described situations. This choice permits controlled variation of vignette
treatments without assigning demographic identities or personal histories to the
model. The original cases also differ in the normative direction of the response:
endorsement of coercive interrogation is socially disapproved, whereas charitable
giving is socially approved. We use this contrast to investigate whether systemprompt stimuli have similar or domain-speciﬁc effects.
Hassner: coercive interrogation. Following [Hassner, 2023], models evaluate the use of sleep deprivation in interrogating a terrorism suspect. The four
conditions are a base case, modest expected intelligence value, protracted duration, and their combination. The response is binary (support versus opposition).
Opposition is treated as the normatively approved response direction for descriptive comparisons, not as a veriﬁed measure of an individual respondent’s latent
attitude.
Savary and Goldsmith: charitable donation. Following [Savary &
Goldsmith, 2020], models report willingness to donate $5 on a ﬁve-point scale.
The three vignette conditions are private donation, public recognition, and public
recognition combined with an additional social beneﬁt. Greater stated willingness
is the normatively approved response direction. The public-recognition treatment
is also substantively important because it may alter the interpretation of donation
as altruism or reputational signaling.
Bolsen et al.: energy conservation. The third experiment adapts the energyconservation framing design of [Bolsen et al., 2014]. Its nine treatment cells
combine attribution of responsibility (government, individuals, or neither) with
economic-cost, environmental-beneﬁt, or no consequence framing. Models rate
likelihood of investing in home insulation or weatherization on a seven-point scale.
We examine original treatment-versus-control comparisons as well as attribution7

by-consequence contrasts. The dependent variable measures stated willingness,
not observed investment.
Corbacho et al.: bribery and institutional information. The fourth experiment adapts [Corbacho et al., 2016], comparing a control with information about
corruption prevalence and an institutional-inefﬁciency placebo. Respondents indicate whether they would offer a bribe to avoid a ﬁne, selecting True, False,
or Prefer not to respond. These are three nominal outcomes; refusal is not interchangeable with unwillingness. The source paper reports direct-response and
privacy-adjusted comparisons with different estimands. Our prompt adaptation
does not reproduce its original crosswise-response procedure.
Across all four experiments, human source outcomes, stakes, and elicitation
procedures differ, so directional comparisons are more defensible than pooling
numerical effects across studies.
Published human treatment contrasts provide an external benchmark. They
are not assumed to be reproduced by every model, and matching a treatment direction does not imply that the full human response distribution or the underlying
psychological mechanism has been reproduced.
3.2. Prompt structure and unit of observation
The original vignette treatment enters through the user-facing question and
response options. The system message speciﬁes the survey-response task; experimental system-prompt stimuli are added to this instruction layer. No demographic
persona is assigned in the principal behavioral design. The exact template and
verbatim stimulus strings must be preserved from the generation conﬁgurations
before the ﬁnal manuscript is frozen.
Each generation produces a ﬁnal answer and, where available, a modelgenerated reasoning trace associated with the same response event. These are
separate analytical outputs, not independent observations. Repeated generations
under a ﬁxed model–experiment–treatment–stimulus conﬁguration measure
response variation within that conﬁguration; they do not represent independent
human respondents. We distinguish the run (one generation) from the design
cell (a speciﬁed combination of model, vignette treatment, stimulus, and relevant
generation settings). Statistical uncertainty must account for this nested structure
8

rather than treating all completions as exchangeable people.
3.3. Models, decoding, and analytical samples
The behavioral corpus uses four API-accessed reasoning-capable models:
moonshotai/kimi-k2-thinking, z-ai/glm-4.6, qwen/qwen3.5-9b, and
minimax/minimax-m2.5. These models were selected pragmatically for the
availability of ﬁnal answers and reasoning traces; they do not constitute a representative sample of all LLMs. A separate local Qwen3-8B is used for hidden-state
analysis and is not pooled with the API behavioral sample.
The principal generation regime uses temperature T = 0.01, with a separate
no-stimulus comparison at T = 1.0. Low temperature improves repeatability but
may compress within-cell variation; the temperature comparison is a decoding
sensitivity check rather than an independent human benchmark.
The existing manuscript reports 15,680 raw runs, 12,434 retained rows after preprocessing, and 11,040 observations in the main T = 0.01 analysis. These
are reported historical totals, not ﬁgures independently recomputed for this editorial draft. Final counts, exclusion rules, rerun handling, and cell-level coverage
must be reconciled against raw data and analysis scripts before submission. In
particular, the extended H–M stimulus family is not covered by all four API models; analyses of these conditions must identify the actual model subset and avoid
unqualiﬁed cross-model generalization.

4. System-Prompt Stimuli
4.1. Conceptual role
System-prompt stimuli modify the instructions surrounding a ﬁxed vignette.
They are intended to discourage normatively polished or sanitized answers, but
their observed effects are described here as stimulus-induced response shifts, not
as established reductions in social desirability bias. The direction of a shift is evaluated relative to the normatively approved response direction in each case. A shift
away from that direction does not, on its own, establish greater correspondence
with human responses.
The historically implemented stimulus set includes the no-stimulus baseline,
9

two core conditions (A and C), and the extended H–M family. A emphasizes the
costs of sanitized answers; C includes an explicit anti-overcorrection qualiﬁcation. The H–M family varies the stated target of consequences or beneﬁts (science
versus a company; personal or institutional beneﬁt versus public good) and the
valence of the framing (negative consequences versus positive beneﬁts). These labels describe intended prompt functions, not independently identiﬁed causal mechanisms.

4.3. Implemented stimulus conditions in the new experiments
Bolsen and Corbacho each use A, C, N0, N−, and N+ prompts. N0 explicitly instructs the model to respond with neutral realism; it is not the historical
no-incentive condition. Similarly labeled prompts should be compared using the
actual message text rather than their short identiﬁers alone. Therefore the new N0referenced comparisons and the older unstimulated-baseline comparisons have different reference conditions, and are not treated as a fully matched four-experiment
factorial experiment.
4.4. Analytical contrasts
The behavioral analysis distinguishes three comparisons: (i) original
vignette-treatment contrasts within a ﬁxed prompt condition; (ii) stimulusinduced shifts relative to a no-stimulus or explicitly speciﬁed reference condition;
and (iii) changes in original treatment contrasts across stimulus conditions.
Attribute-level comparisons are interpreted as conditional associations unless the
compared prompts isolate the attribute of interest and have sufﬁcient balanced
coverage. Reasoning annotations and hidden-state measures are examined
as complementary outcomes, with their separate validity and generalizability
limitations stated in the relevant methods sections.

5. Analytical Strategy
The unit is one model generation within an experiment–treatment–stimulus
cell. Repeated low-temperature completions are not independent human respondents; all uncertainty estimates are interpreted conditionally on the generation and
sampling protocols.
5.1. Behavioral outcomes
Hassner endorsement is binary (logit and penalized-logit sensitivity); Savary
uses a ﬁve-point willingness score (OLS, ordinal, and threshold sensitivities);
Bolsen uses a seven-point investment scale (cell means and ordinal distributions).
Corbacho has three nominal outcomes—True, False, and refusal—which are never
collapsed into an ordered score. We estimate original vignette-treatment contrasts
11

within stimulus and model, stimulus shifts within original treatments, and their
interactions. The principal reference is historical no stimulus, fresh none, or new
neutral realism N0, as appropriate; these are distinct conditions.
Pairwise Holm corrections and broader Benjamini–Hochberg adjustments
address multiplicity. Historical models use heteroskedasticity-robust and designcell-clustered checks. Low-temperature repetitions, quasi-separation and saturated cells constrain conventional uncertainty estimates. The new-experiment analysis uses model-speciﬁc cell contrasts, ordinal/nominal distribution checks and
conditional block resampling; the latter does not yield uncertainty for a human
population. Historical H–M prompt-attribute regressions are exploratory because
the observed prompt packages do not isolate framing valence or beneﬁciary in a
full factorial. Fresh none versus N-series comparisons may also reﬂect differences
in run timing and provider routing.
5.2. Verbalized reasoning
The historical annotation pipeline retained 8,638 records; its categories encode three-level argument centrality, signed direction where deﬁned, and style. A
27-trace expert-coded subset shows uneven agreement. The new Bolsen/Corbacho
batch contains 1,398 eligible traces over selected original treatments, and instead
encodes binary category presence with signed direction where applicable. These
schemes are not pooled as if they measured identical centrality. We estimate
stimulus-related changes in category prevalence and, separately, argument direction among occurrences; undeﬁned direction is not assigned zero. Within-cell regressions relate category presence to ﬁnal outcomes with model, treatment, stimulus and version controls. Associations between co-generated traces and responses
are not causal mediation. For rare and near-constant categories, estimates can be
unidentiﬁed or imprecise.
5.3. Local hidden-state exploration
A separate Qwen3-8B study records 37-layer, 4,096-dimensional last-token
representations for 98 runs under a different prompt inventory. Layer-wise distances, centered kernel alignment, matched trajectory contrasts, and predictive
probes are descriptive diagnostics, not direct evidence of a mechanism in the API
models. The study distinguishes observable shifts, recovery of published human
12

treatment effects, and broader human validity throughout.

6. Results
6.1. Final answers: heterogeneous models and systematic prompt shifts
The four API models differ substantially in observed answers. Across the
reported T = 0.01 analytical sample, Hassner endorsement rates range from 10.3%
(Minimax) to 67.5% (GLM-4.6), while Savary mean willingness ranges from 3.33
(Kimi) to 3.73 (Qwen). The Hassner treatment-by-model speciﬁcation also improves model ﬁt (McFadden pseudo-R2 : 0.507 to 0.543; ∆AIC = −304). Model
identity therefore matters for both response levels and sensitivity to vignette manipulations. These descriptive rates pool different available stimulus conditions
and should not be read as balanced model comparisons.
In the no-stimulus Hassner condition, support for sleep deprivation decreases from 44.6% in the control to 19.3% with protracted duration, 12.2% with
modest intelligence value, and 1.0% with both modiﬁcations. These directions
agree with the human experimental benchmark. In Savary, by contrast, the LLM
mean rises from 3.39 in the private condition to 3.73 with public recognition
and 4.09 with added social beneﬁt. The increment associated with social beneﬁt
is directionally consistent with the source experiment, but the private-to-public
increase reverses the human ﬁnding: public recognition reduced willingness
in the original study. The benchmark therefore reveals domain-speciﬁc limits
of directional replication rather than a general success or failure of synthetic
respondents.
All eight historical system-prompt stimuli are associated with shifts away
from the normatively approved response direction in the pooled descriptive comparisons. In Hassner, the no-stimulus endorsement rate is 19.1%; it is 41.6% under
C, 72.1% under A, and ranges from 56.4% to 90.0% across H–M. In Savary, mean
willingness is 3.74 without a stimulus, 3.44 under C, 3.32 under A, and 3.14–
3.35 across H–M (Table 5). These are socially desirable response patterns and
stimulus-induced response shifts, not direct estimates of human-style SDB or its
mitigation.
The comparisons require a coverage qualiﬁcation: H–M were ﬁelded only
on Kimi and GLM-4.6, while the baseline and core conditions have broader model
13

coverage. Thus the displayed pooled means are useful descriptive summaries, but
differences involving H–M are not automatically like-for-like causal estimates. In
the historical attribute regression restricted to the two-model subsample, positiveframed L/M prompts are associated with substantially higher Hassner endorsement than negative-framed H–K (reported OR ≈ 15, p < 0.001) and a smaller
decrease in Savary willingness (reported β = −0.13, p = 0.02). Because the observed prompts do not form a complete matched factorial, these are associations
between prompt packages, not isolated effects of framing valence. Nonsigniﬁcant
recipient and beneﬁciary coefﬁcients do not establish that those attributes are inert.
Treatment-by-stimulus contrasts also vary: the original analysis reports
moderation in some conditions, without reversal of the sign of the original
Hassner treatment contrasts. In Savary, the private-to-public contrast remains
positive across the eight historical stimuli (reported range +0.05 to +0.26),
so none reproduces the human reversal under the tested settings. This result
identiﬁes a limitation of the observed prompts, not a general impossibility of
eliciting self-signaling-related reasoning.
Historical-data reconciliation. Reapplying Notebook 05 preprocessing to
the available master table yields 11,040 observations in 168 model–treatment–
stimulus cells; 16 cell estimates differ from an earlier export. The pooled baseline
means above are consistent with the reconstruction. Model-speciﬁc no-stimulus
Hassner modest-intelligence contrasts are −0.785 (GLM) and −0.442 (Kimi);
Savary public-minus-private contrasts are +0.238 and +0.400. Directions remain
unchanged. The exact provenance of the notebook’s original input and some
run-temperature metadata remains unresolved.
6.2. Fresh none replication: Hassner and Savary
A separate low-temperature October 2026 run without an additional incentive section produced 698 valid answers from 700 requests. In Hassner, GLM control endorsement is 1.000, falling to 0.673 (duration), 0.360 (modest intelligence),
and 0.040 (both); Kimi’s corresponding rates are 0.571, 0.140, 0, and 0. In Savary,
private/public/social-beneﬁt means are 3.48/3.92/4.42 (GLM) and 3.34/3.66/4.00
(Kimi). Both studies preserve the historical treatment-effect directions, including
Savary’s public-recognition reversal relative to humans. The new none sample is

not pooled with historical runs; its contrasts against later N-series runs are descriptive because inference-provider routing and execution time differed.
6.3. N-series behavioral extension: Hassner and Savary
The N0/N−/N+ extension yields 2,065 valid answers from 2,100 requests
(Kimi 1,043; GLM 1,022). In Savary, N0 relative to the separate none run
lowers mean willingness by 0.15 points for Kimi (95% CI [−0.23, −0.06], Holm
p = 0.013) and by 0.12 for GLM (Holm p = 0.339). Compared with N0, both
additional framings raise Kimi’s mean by about 0.09, but lower GLM’s by 0.14
(N−) and 0.21 (N+). The GLM N+ shift is −0.213 ([−0.320, −0.107], Holm
p = 0.00117); the corresponding GLM–Kimi difference in shifts is −0.307
([−0.447, −0.167]).
In Hassner, mean N+–N0 changes are −6 percentage points (pp) for Kimi
(95% CI [−11.65, 0.18]; Holm p = 0.335) and +4 pp for GLM (Holm p = 1).
However, Kimi’s control endorsement falls from 72% to 46%, an exploratory −26
pp local shift that changes treatment contrasts. Twenty-seven transport failures
left only 23 of 50 valid GLM duration responses under N−. The Kimi mean
contrast attenuates to about −2 pp after provider adjustment. Thus near-symmetric
framings do not yield a universal effect, and cross-run contrasts cannot be assigned
solely to prompt wording.
6.4. Additional experiments: Bolsen and Corbacho
Sample and comparability. The two new experiments contain 6,034 saved generations, 6,000 of which satisfy parsing and scale checks. GLM-4.6 and Kimi-K2Thinking cover the full treatment-by-stimulus design; Qwen3.5-9B contributes
only 33 valid Bolsen control/A responses and is excluded from the principal comparisons. The realized prompt conditions are A, C, N0, N−, and N+. N0 is a neutral realism instruction, not the historical no-incentive baseline; these references
must not be equated.
Bolsen: partial directional replication and model heterogeneity. Under N0,
both models’ control means are 5.000 on the seven-point investment scale. Government attribution lowers investment likelihood by 0.840 points for GLM and
1.800 for Kimi, compared with approximately −0.370 in the published human
15

contrast. Cost framing lowers it by 1.060 and 0.600 points, compared with a
published contrast of approximately −0.360. The combined individual-attribution
and environmental-beneﬁt condition is only 0.020 points above control for either
model, versus approximately +0.450 in the human study. Agreement in sign thus
coexists with sizeable discrepancies in magnitude (Table 3). Published human contrasts are reconstructed from rounded means, and exact comparability is limited.
The factorial pattern is also heterogeneous. At N0, the individual-byenvironment interaction is 0.000 for GLM and −0.180 for Kimi, against
approximately +0.120 in the reported human means. Averaging equally across
the nine treatments, A changes GLM’s mean by +0.168 relative to N0 but
Kimi’s by −0.665; the corresponding C shifts are +0.257 and −0.097. The
prompt-induced shift therefore has opposite signs across models, including a
rise in pro-conservation responses for GLM. In Kimi, the cost-minus-control
contrast changes from −0.600 under N0 to +0.080 under A. These shifts neither
guarantee greater human correspondence nor support a uniform de-sanitization
claim.
Corbacho: three response outcomes and placebo sensitivity. Under N0,
every valid GLM and Kimi answer in all three treatment groups is False; the
observed treatment differences are zero. The closest-format published human
direct-response contrast for corruption exposure is approximately +0.030, with
a reported conﬁdence interval reaching approximately zero. A larger published
privacy-adjusted joint contrast (+0.080) measures a different quantity and cannot
be substituted. Human and synthetic elicitation procedures differ.
For Kimi under A, the proportions choosing True are 0.560 in control,
0.694 under corruption information, and 0.840 under the institutional-inefﬁciency
placebo; the corresponding refusals are 0.380, 0.306, and 0.160. Under C, True
proportions are 0.100, 0.300, and 0.447, with refusals of 0.440, 0.220, and 0.234.
Thus the corruption-minus-placebo True contrast is approximately −0.146 under
A and −0.147 under C, with the placebo producing more afﬁrmative answers
than the corruption treatment. The treatment-balanced True proportion under A is
0.054 for GLM but 0.698 for Kimi; no GLM refusals appear under A. Refusals
are retained as a separate nominal response outcome, not coded as opposition or
as an ordinal midpoint (Table 4).

The new negative- and positive-consequence stimuli. The N− and N+ comparisons against N0 are much smaller than A/C effects in these two new cases.
In Bolsen, treatment-balanced mean shifts under N− are +0.038 (GLM) and
+0.104 (Kimi) scale points, compared with −0.014 and −0.011 under N+. Thus
the N+ minus N− contrasts are −0.051 and −0.115, respectively. Under conditional block-of-ten resampling, the latter contrast has a reference interval of
[−0.186, −0.045] for Kimi but [−0.112, 0.008] for GLM; these are not humanpopulation conﬁdence intervals. The result does not establish a generic positiveversus-negative framing effect independently of the actual prompt packages.
In Corbacho, GLM has no observed True or refusal responses under either
N− or N+ in the balanced comparisons. Kimi’s True share changes by +0.033
(3.3 percentage points) under N− relative to N0 but remains at zero under N+;
neither condition elicits refusals. Hence N+ minus N− in Kimi is −0.033 for True,
with a conditional block-of-ten reference interval [−0.053, −0.013]. In contrast,
under A and C the same model produces large afﬁrmative and refusal shares. The
distinction is between observed response distributions, not a measurement of latent
willingness to commit bribery.
Four-case interpretation. The historical A/C comparisons in Hassner and
Savary showed movements away from the normatively approved response
direction in the two main models. Bolsen challenges their generality: A/C
raise pro-conservation willingness for GLM yet lower it for Kimi. Corbacho
adds a qualitatively different pattern: response saturation under N0, pronounced
Kimi-speciﬁc changes under A/C, nonresponse, and a larger placebo response
than corruption-information response. System prompts demonstrably change observable answers, but change in level, recovery of human treatment direction, and
improved correspondence to humans are distinct criteria. New-case resampling
intervals are conditional descriptive diagnostics, not population-level signiﬁcance
tests.
6.5. Verbalized reasoning: changes in content and argumentative direction
The annotation results indicate that stimuli change the text models produce
while selecting answers. Explicit overcorrection_concern appears in approximately 9% of no-stimulus traces and 72–76% under A/C (expert-validation κ =
17

0.64, n = 27). The prevalence of explicitly rational-deliberative style rises from
0.67 to 0.86, and acknowledgement of multiple defensible positions from 0.57 to
0.80. The latter style codes were not included in the small human-validation set
and should be treated as exploratory. These changes describe generated text, not
independently veriﬁed cognitive awareness.
The A–C comparison is especially informative in Hassner. Endorsement is
72.1% under A versus 41.6% under C, while several coded category-prevalence
differences are small. Direction codes, however, differ markedly: signed presence
of ratio_of_goals_and_means changes from −0.01 under C to +0.42 under A,
majority_reliance from +0.06 to +0.41, and cruelty from −0.68 to −0.34
(Table 6). The ﬁrst of these codes was not covered by expert validation; the others
have limited or category-speciﬁc reliability evidence. The pattern is consistent
with a shift in the polarity of verbalized arguments, but it does not show that these
arguments mediate the stimulus effect.
In Savary, both A and C strongly increase annotation of personal costs and
beneﬁts, while their mean ﬁnal answers remain comparatively close (3.32 and
3.44). Across both domains, the annotation vocabulary therefore responds to
prompts, but the relation between coded text and the answer is domain-dependent.
A separability benchmark classiﬁes stimulus-present versus no-stimulus
traces from annotation features at reported cross-validated ROC-AUC 0.96,
compared with 0.68 for low- versus high-temperature no-stimulus traces. The
original analysis also reports that the contrast survives exclusion of directly
stimulus-linked codes. This demonstrates that the two classiﬁcation tasks have
different separability under the chosen features and samples; it does not by itself
measure relative causal impact on internal reasoning.
The annotation instrument has uneven validation. In a 27-trace expertcoded subset, agreement is high for cruelty and personal_cost_burden (both
κ = 1.00 for causality coding) and moderate-to-substantial for overcorrection_
concern (κ = 0.64), whereas several psychological categories have near-zero
agreement (Table 7). Interpretations of subtle self-presentational categories must
therefore remain tentative.

6.6. Consequence framing and reasoning in Hassner and Savary
The completed N-series annotation contains 840 traces, 480 for Hassner
and 360 for Savary (20 per design cell). Unlike historical centrality coding, these
categories have binary presence indicators. In Hassner, the N−–N0 increase in
incentive_awareness is 18.75 percentage points (pp; 95% CI [11.74, 26.10]),
and the N+–N0 increase is 22.50 pp ([15.17, 29.72]). These mentions reﬂect, at
least partly, consequences explicitly stated in the instructions. The N−–N0 change
in emotional_reasoning is -13.75 pp in GLM ([-24.59, -2.90]) but +21.25 pp in
Kimi ([8.02, 32.94]).
In Savary, incentive_awareness increases by 23.33 pp under N+ relative to N0 ([14.93, 31.15]), although its cluster-based multiple-testing-adjusted
result is not signiﬁcant (q = 0.558). The N+–N0 contrast for possibility_of_
different_opinions differs between models by 31.67 pp (GLM minus Kimi;
95% CI [18.54, 44.80], q = 0.0142). Within matched design cells, source_
reliance is positively associated with donation willingness (normalized ordinalrank difference +0.100, 95% CI [0.054, 0.146], BH q = 0.00486). These are associations, not mediation estimates. For Hassner incentive_awareness, only 10
of 66 occurrences have known argumentative direction; sign changes relative to
N0 cannot be estimated when the category is absent at baseline.
6.7. Reasoning in Bolsen and Corbacho: prevalence and direction
The analyzed sample covers 1,398 eligible traces (799 Bolsen; 599 Corbacho), two models, all ﬁve system stimuli, and only four of Bolsen’s nine original
treatments. The new codebook records category presence as binary (0/1), not the
historical three-level centrality scale; ﬁndings from the two annotation pipelines
are therefore reported separately. Regression estimates adjust for original treatment, model, and annotation version.
Compared with N0, A and C markedly increase the prevalence of
overcorrection_concern: by 50.7 and 51.3 percentage points (pp) in Bolsen,
and 70.8 and 57.9 pp in Corbacho, respectively (all BH-adjusted q ≤ 0.006). This
category closely reﬂects the A/C instructions, so greater prevalence need not
represent deeper or more human-like deliberation. Domain-speciﬁc differences
are also visible: C increases economic_payback in Bolsen by 25.6 pp (95%
19

CI [12.2, 39.0], q = 0.006), whereas A increases corruption_normalization
in Corbacho by 49.1 pp ([36.6, 61.6]; q < 0.001). The corresponding N−/N+
prevalence contrasts are generally smaller and less precise. There is pronounced
model heterogeneity: the Bolsen A–N0 increase in overcorrection_concern is
2.6 pp for GLM and 98.7 pp for Kimi. These contrasts measure annotated textual
features, not causal mechanisms.
The direction analysis separates category occurrence from the sign of an argument among traces where it occurs. Signs refer to support for investing (Bolsen)
or bribery (Corbacho), never automatically to the ﬁnal answer. Of 14 signed
Bolsen and 12 signed Corbacho categories, none has a newly estimated pooled
direction effect or model interaction surviving Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment
(q < 0.05). For example, in Bolsen A raises majority_reliance prevalence by
20.1 pp, while the conditional share of positively signed instances is estimated to
fall by 35.6 pp (95% CI [−70.3, −1.0]; q = 0.728). This exploratory contrast illustrates why increased occurrence cannot be equated with more positive argumentative direction. Many categories have nearly invariant signs or insufﬁcient comparable N0 occurrences; neither a zero effect nor a historical signed-importance score
is imputed. Annotation validity, restricted treatment coverage, and conditioning
on category presence further limit inference.
6.8. Associations between reasoning annotations and answers
Adding annotation features increases reported explanatory ﬁt in Savary from
R2 = 0.419 to 0.482 (∆R2 = 0.063). Overcorrection concern is negatively associated with willingness (β = −0.047, p = 0.012), while positively directed
personal-cost and private-interest codes are associated with higher willingness
(both reported β = +0.046). A gradient-boosting model also outperforms the linear benchmark in cross-validation (reported R2 = 0.558 versus 0.475). These patterns indicate predictive information in verbalized reasoning beyond the speciﬁed
treatment, stimulus, and model controls, but do not demonstrate that the generated
text is causally upstream of the answer.
For Hassner, the original non-tautological annotation speciﬁcation reports
a smaller increment in ﬁt (∆R2 = 0.052). Positive directions of majority-reliance
and means–ends arguments are associated with endorsement, while overcorrection
concern itself is not a signiﬁcant predictor (p = 0.92). Taken together, the two
20

experiments suggest that the observable association between reasoning categories
and ﬁnal responses varies by domain. The small expert-validation sample and
co-generation of traces and answers limit stronger process claims.
Associations in the new experiments. Within matched model–originaltreatment–stimulus cells, the presence of economic_payback in Bolsen is
associated with a +0.076 difference on the normalized ordinal midrank outcome
(95% CI [0.040, 0.113], multiplicity-adjusted q = 0.003); a mean-score sensitivity
estimate is +0.204 points ([0.111, 0.297]). These are associations between jointly
generated text and answers, not evidence of mediation. In Corbacho, positive
associations of some private-interest and means–ends categories with True do not
survive multiplicity adjustment, and refusals remain a distinct nominal outcome.
Low within-cell variation, uneven semantic validation, and the two annotation
versions constrain these estimates.
6.9. Exploratory hidden-state results
The local Qwen3-8B analysis covers 98 runs (56 Hassner and 42 Savary)
under a different stimulus inventory from the API study. In matched comparisons,
the reported cumulative hidden-state trajectory is shorter under stimulus conditions than without a stimulus (3,370 versus 3,444 distance units; paired Wilcoxon
p < 0.001). This is a geometric difference across layer representations, not a direct
measure of computational effort or deliberation.
The between-/within-condition separation statistic peaks near layers 20–21
in the reported comparisons. Linear CKA remains above 0.94 across layers (reported L20 CKA = 0.994), indicating broadly similar representational geometry
despite detectable condition differences. These summaries do not uniquely identify a translation mechanism or a semantic processing stage.
The local model also exhibits a representational–behavioral dissociation:
stimulus-related hidden-state differences are detectable even where its Hassner
responses are near-saturated (reported P(Yes) ≈ 0.998). A treatment-status probe
performs poorly under leave-one-group-out validation despite higher standard
cross-validation performance, cautioning against scenario-speciﬁc leakage. For
Savary, a condition-residualized ridge probe reaches a reported leave-one-out
R2 = 0.665 at layer 27. The distinct layer peaks are descriptive: they do not
21

establish when a decision is causally formed. Small samples, high-dimensional
activations, and the separate model architecture constrain generalization.
6.10. Synthesis
Three results are most robust as a descriptive account of the historical corpus. First, model responses vary substantially across model families and between
the two social domains; the Savary public-recognition contrast reverses the human
benchmark. Second, system-prompt stimuli consistently accompany shifts away
from the normatively approved response direction in the reported descriptive comparisons, while their apparent magnitude depends on prompt condition and model
coverage. Third, stimuli are associated with changes in verbalized reasoning and
with measurable hidden-state differences in a separate local model, but neither
track alone identiﬁes the mechanism producing ﬁnal answers.
The historical ﬁndings support the study of system prompts as consequential experimental inputs, not as neutral wrappers. They do not establish that steering responses away from social approval makes LLMs more human-like, that the
observed attribute associations isolate framing valence, or that verbalized and representational measures constitute a causal chain. The additional Bolsen and Corbacho results above reveal further model- and domain-speciﬁc heterogeneity.

8. Discussion
Across four adapted vignette experiments, LLM treatment sensitivity varies
by domain, model, and surrounding system instructions. Under the historical nostimulus condition, Hassner treatment contrasts have the same direction as in the
human study, whereas the Savary public-recognition contrast has the opposite sign.
Bolsen shows partial directional correspondence but often different magnitudes.
Corbacho produces uniformly negative answers under the neutral-realism reference N0; the corresponding human direct-response contrast is small and imprecisely estimated, limiting claims about replication failure.
The original A/C stimuli move Hassner and Savary answers away from the
normatively approved response direction. This pattern is not general across the
additional cases: relative to N0, A raises Bolsen energy-conservation willingness
in GLM but lowers it in Kimi. In Corbacho, A/C change Kimi’s afﬁrmative and
refusal shares substantially, and the institutional-inefﬁciency placebo elicits more
afﬁrmative answers than the corruption-information treatment. The N−/N+ shifts
are generally modest on average, but Savary shows opposite directions in GLM
and Kimi, and Hassner exhibits a substantial local control-cell change despite a
small pooled shift. These patterns establish prompt sensitivity, not greater ﬁdelity
to humans or a uniform reduction in social desirability bias. N0 includes a realism
instruction and is not equivalent to historical no stimulus.
Classical gain–loss framing effects therefore do not imply that consequence
framing in a system prompt must shift synthetic answers. The generally small
N−/N+ contrasts are compatible with context-dependent framing effects, but
do not establish equivalence or prove a zero effect. Saturated N0 responses
in Corbacho also limit observable shifts, while N0 itself contains a realism
instruction. Historical H–M comparisons suggest a valence association, but their
incomplete prompt factorial cannot isolate valence causally. In the new cases,
A/C produce large changes in annotated category prevalence and strongly heterogeneous GLM/Kimi patterns. Bolsen payback arguments are associated with
higher investment ratings within matched cells, whereas new direction contrasts
do not survive multiplicity adjustment. Category presence, argumentative sign,
and response association measure different features; none establishes mediation.
Exploratory hidden-state differences in a separate local Qwen3-8B likewise
26

cannot explain the API models’ responses. LLM simulations may help stress-test
experimental wording, but comparisons against human treatment benchmarks
remain necessary.

9. Scope and Limitations
The four cases cover selected socially sensitive vignettes, not representative human survey populations. Model coverage is unequal: the historical H–M
family is limited to GLM and Kimi, and the principal new-experiment comparisons likewise use those two models. Repeated low-temperature generations are
not independent human respondents. Corbacho’s three nominal answers require
separate treatment of refusal; its human privacy-adjusted estimate is not directly
equivalent to our direct-response outcome. The N0 reference and historical nostimulus baseline differ, as do some original vignette procedures. New none and
N-series runs were executed sequentially with mixed API-provider routing, and 27
transport errors cluster in one GLM/Hassner/N− cell; neither cross-run wording
effects nor local interactions are fully isolated.
The historical reasoning codebook was checked against only 27 expertcoded traces, with highly uneven agreement across categories. The new binary
presence annotations lack equivalent human-gold validation, and the Bolsen
annotations cover only selected original treatments. Reasoning-answer associations are predictive, not causal. Hidden-state measurements concern 98 runs of
a different, local Qwen3-8B checkpoint; layer-wise geometry and probing are
diagnostic rather than mechanistic proof. Historical H–M prompt wording has
not been independently authenticated verbatim. Finally, the historical numerical
reconciliation uses an available master-table copy and does not establish identity
with the original notebook input.

10. Conclusion
System prompts materially affect synthetic experimental answers, but the
direction and implications of those effects depend on task and model. Agreement
with some human treatment contrasts coexists with reversals, attenuated effects,
response saturation, and placebo sensitivity. Historical traces and separate-model
27

representations provide complementary correlational evidence, not a validated
causal pathway. Consequently, moving answers away from socially approved positions must not be equated with improved human realism. LLMs are potentially
useful for experimental stress-testing, but not as unvalidated substitutes for human
respondents.
