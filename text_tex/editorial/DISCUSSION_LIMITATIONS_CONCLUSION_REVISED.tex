\section{Discussion}

The findings show that system-prompt instructions are substantive experimental inputs in LLM-based vignette studies. Across two socially sensitive domains, the historical stimuli are associated with shifts away from the normatively approved response direction. Yet the direction of this movement is not a measure of increased human realism. A model may change its answer because it follows a new instruction, responds to the described stakes, or changes how it frames the vignette. These possibilities cannot be separated fully by the historical design.

The human benchmarks help identify the limits of interpreting such shifts. In Hassner, the no-stimulus model responses reproduce the direction of the original vignette-treatment contrasts. In Savary and Goldsmith, however, public recognition increases stated donation willingness in the LLM sample, whereas the original human studies report a decrease. One possible interpretation is that the models emphasize the incentive of public recognition without reproducing the self-signaling concerns described in the source study. This remains an interpretation rather than a demonstrated absence of that mechanism: neither the final answer nor the generated reasoning trace directly reveals the model's latent decision process. Importantly, none of the historical stimuli reverses the observed LLM public-recognition contrast under the tested settings.

The stimulus inventory also suggests that \emph{how} consequences are framed may matter. Positive-framed L/M conditions are associated with larger shifts than the negative-framed H--K conditions in the historical two-model comparisons, particularly in Hassner. But because these prompts do not form a complete matched factorial, their differences cannot be attributed uniquely to framing valence. The planned N0, negative-research-consequences, and positive-research-consequences conditions address a narrower comparison with a shared base instruction. Their results are not yet part of the present evidence.

The three analytical levels add complementary information without establishing a causal sequence. Final answers identify behavioral shifts. Annotation shows that stimuli coincide with changes in verbalized argumentative content and direction, including increased explicit concern about overly sanitized responses. Local hidden-state measurements show that prompt conditions can be geometrically distinguishable even when final answers change little. These are distinct observations, made partly on different models. They should not be collapsed into a claim that a specific hidden-state displacement causes a particular argument, which then causes the final answer.

For synthetic piloting, the practical implication is conditional rather than uniformly positive or negative. LLMs can expose sensitivity to vignette treatments and to the surrounding instruction environment. They cannot be assumed to reproduce human response distributions, human motivational mechanisms, or even the direction of every human treatment effect. Consequently, prompt specifications, model identity, and cross-condition coverage belong in the experimental design and reporting, not merely in implementation notes.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

The study covers two attitude-expression vignettes with contrasting normative contexts, not the broader universe of sensitive self-report or survey-methodology designs. The four API models were selected pragmatically and differ in baseline response patterns and treatment sensitivity; the stimulus inventory is unbalanced across them. In particular, H--M are available only for Kimi and GLM-4.6, so pooled comparisons across the full stimulus inventory require caution. Repeated generations within the same design cell are not independent respondents, and near-deterministic decoding further limits within-cell variation. Reported inference must be read in light of the design-cell clustering and quasi-separated binary outcomes.

The distinction between \emph{socially desirable response patterns} and human \emph{social desirability bias} is central. The study does not directly identify impression management, self-deception, or a uniquely safety-alignment-driven mechanism in LLMs. Nor does a shift away from a normatively approved response establish greater human correspondence. Published human studies provide external treatment benchmarks, but differ from the LLM task in respondent population, incentives, and in some cases outcome measurement.

The reasoning annotation is generated by an LLM coder and validated on only 27 expert-coded traces in the main analysis. Reliability varies substantially across categories, especially for subtle psychological constructs. Moreover, reasoning traces and final answers are co-generated, so predictive relationships between them cannot be interpreted as causal mediation. The MoralLens model is a separate prospective annotation tool trained primarily to reproduce teacher labels, not independent human ground truth; its teacher-agreement scores do not resolve the main annotation-validity limitations.

Hidden-state findings are restricted to 98 runs of a separate local Qwen3-8B model and a different stimulus set. Last-token, layer-wise geometry and probe accuracy cannot by themselves establish a computational mechanism. The local model's near-saturated Hassner outcomes further restrict behavioral comparisons. Finally, the exact historical H--M wording is represented in the available project materials by semantic reconstruction; these adopted formulations should not be described as independently authenticated verbatim generation prompts.

\section{Conclusion}

This study examined how system-prompt stimuli affect large language models in socially sensitive vignette experiments at three observational levels: final answers, verbalized reasoning, and hidden-state representations. The historical behavioral corpus shows substantial heterogeneity between models, directional agreement with the Hassner human benchmark, and a reversal of the Savary public-recognition effect. Across the observed stimulus conditions, model answers shift away from the normatively approved response direction in both domains, although unequal model coverage and the incomplete stimulus design constrain attribute-level interpretations.

The annotation results show systematic changes in verbalized arguments, particularly explicit concern about overly sanitized answers and differences in argumentative direction between the A and C prompts. The separate local-model analysis finds detectable hidden-state differences that do not map straightforwardly onto behavioral change. Together, these findings support a multi-level description of prompt sensitivity, not a validated causal account of the internal response process.

The contribution is therefore methodological as well as empirical: system prompts should be treated as experimental factors whose wording and coverage can materially alter conclusions drawn from LLM simulations. Human experimental benchmarks remain essential, and neither movement away from socially approved answers nor richer verbalized reasoning is sufficient evidence of bias reduction or improved human validity. The planned matched N0/negative/positive comparison will provide a more controlled test of consequence framing; until those runs are analyzed, its outcomes remain open.
